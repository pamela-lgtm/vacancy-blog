Lake Nona Landlords: Vacancy Doesn’t Send a Bill. It Still Costs You.

A higher asking rent can quietly produce a lower return. Here is the simple math every landlord should know.

You open the electric bill. You pay the property taxes. You notice the insurance premium.

But an empty rental never sends an invoice for the rent you didn’t collect.

That doesn’t make vacancy free.

For Lake Nona landlords, one of the most expensive pricing decisions can sound perfectly reasonable: “Let’s hold out a little longer. I don’t want to leave money on the table.”

The question is how much money leaves the table while you wait.

For every example below, assume the property is vacant, show-ready and available for immediate occupancy. Vacancy is measured until rent begins, and alternative rents are assumed to be achievable.

The $3,500 rental that effectively earns $2,829

Consider a home advertised at $3,500 per month that sits vacant and ready to rent for 70 days. Assume it then rents for $3,500 and stays occupied for the rest of the first year.

Full-year potential rent$42,000
Rent lost during 70 vacant days−$8,054.79
First-year gross rental income$33,945.21
Effective monthly income over that year$2,828.77

The owner’s effective monthly income has fallen to approximately $2,829, before expenses.

But the prospective renter still sees $3,500.

The renter receives no discount for the owner’s lost income. The owner has absorbed a substantial loss without making the advertised price more competitive.

That is why vacancy can become such a frustrating outcome: the owner collects less, while the renter still faces the same price that hasn’t secured a lease.

Can about 23 days erase the benefit of higher rent?

Yes—in a specific comparison. There is no universal 23-day break-even rule. It depends on the rent you are holding out for and the realistic alternative.

Suppose you could begin collecting $3,275 now, or hold out for $3,500. The higher rent promises an extra $225 per month, or $2,700 over a full year.

At $3,500 per month, each vacant day represents approximately $115.07 in uncollected rent, using a 365-day year.

$2,700 in extra annual rent ÷ $115.07 per vacant day = about 23.5 days.

Wait 24 extra vacant days for $3,500, and the higher-rent strategy has already fallen behind $3,275 collected immediately over the same first-year period.

At that point, you may still sign a $3,500 lease. But the higher monthly number does not erase the income already lost.

The smaller the rent increase you are chasing, the less time you can afford to wait.

*Illustrations assume the lower rent begins immediately and continues for 365 days. The higher rent begins after the additional vacancy and continues through that same year. Break-even days = 365 × (higher monthly rent − lower monthly rent) ÷ higher monthly rent. These figures measure additional vacant days, not a neighborhood average or a guaranteed leasing timeline.

What if that wait becomes 70 days?

In the same example, $3,275 collected for a full year produces $39,300. Waiting 70 vacant days and then collecting $3,500 produces approximately $33,945 during that year.

The lower asking rent produces approximately $5,355 more gross income.

And that comparison does not add the extra turnover-period spending that may accompany a prolonged vacancy. Utilities, lawn care and repeated property visits can make the gap wider. Some ownership costs continue whether the property is occupied or empty.

A practical plan for Lake Nona landlords

Your home competes with the alternatives a renter can choose today. Review comparable homes with similar layouts, condition, garages, outdoor space and included services. Give more weight to actual leased results than to ambitious asking prices that remain available.

  • Start competitively. Let the home’s condition and current alternatives support the price.
  • Make it ready before launch. Clean presentation, accurate photos and convenient showings help qualified renters make a decision.
  • Review the response after the first week. Track qualified inquiries, completed showings, applications and repeated objections.
  • Adjust while the listing is fresh. If the response is weak, address pricing, presentation and access promptly.
  • Keep screening standards consistent. Price competitively to attract qualified applicants while maintaining your resident-selection criteria.

At Verandah, we use a 21-day leasing goal to create urgency around preparation, pricing and follow-through. It is a management target, not a promise or a mathematical deadline that applies to every home.

Your monthly rent goal needs a total-income reality check

It is reasonable to have a monthly rent goal. But that goal should never be evaluated without the cost of reaching it.

Each vacant day is a real financial loss that belongs in your calculation. Once that day passes, its rent is gone. A future tenant does not reimburse you for the weeks the property sat empty. Higher future income may offset that loss over time, but it cannot restore the missed rental days.

Every week the home remains vacant, recalculate what you stand to collect over the same first-year period. Compare the higher rent you hope to achieve with a competitive rent that could begin sooner. Include concessions and extra vacancy-related expenses. Your monthly target should be tested against those numbers, not allowed to obscure them.

A higher rent promise is not a higher return

In our experience, some owners have chosen another management company after receiving a higher proposed rent than our recommendation for a 21-day leasing goal. We have then watched the advertised rent come down in stages while the property remains available for weeks or months.

That is difficult to see because those missed weeks have a lasting financial consequence for the owner. By the time the asking rent reaches a competitive level, the owner may have absorbed more vacancy loss than the hoped-for increase could reasonably justify.

Choosing a manager based on the highest rental quote can become an expensive decision if that quote is not supported by qualified demand. Ask what comparable homes actually leased for, how long they took, and what the plan is if the initial price does not generate qualified applications.

A property manager cannot promise a rent the market will not support. Renters decide which available home offers the best value for their needs. A manager’s preparation, marketing, responsiveness and showing process can help your property compete and support the strongest achievable rent—but a higher quote alone does not create that demand.

Your property manager should be your financial advocate

A professional looking out for your interests should explain the tradeoff clearly, even when the recommendation is a lower monthly rent than you hoped for.

That means giving you an evidence-based starting price, monitoring the response, calculating the cost of continued vacancy and recommending timely adjustments. It also means being candid when the market does not support your target, while maintaining consistent resident-selection standards.

The job is to protect the income you can actually collect. Sometimes that means supporting a higher rent with strong demand. Sometimes it means recommending a lower rent now to avoid a larger loss later.

Before holding out for another $100, $200 or $300 per month, ask: “How many additional vacant days would erase that gain?”
